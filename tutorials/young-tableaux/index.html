Young tableaux¶

The Young-tableau toolkit builds the many-body wavefunctions that label the states of an su(n) representation, takes their inner products, normalizes them, and orthogonalizes a degenerate weight space — the same Gram–Schmidt step you do in quantum mechanics. This is the SU(3) computation from the guided tour made executable; it mirrors demos/03-young-tableaux.wls.

Needs["ClassicalLieAlgebra`"];

The Young symmetrizer¶

The su(3) {1, 1} irrep has a highest-weight tableau of shape \([2,1]\), filled with 1s along the first row and 2s along the second:

Tableau[rows] is the shape, carrying its Young symmetrizer; Psi[i1, i2, ...] is a basis tensor \(|i_1 i_2 \dots\rangle\); and TableauPermute applies the symmetrizer to a tensor. For the column-standard shape {{1,2},{3}} acting on \(|1\,1\,2\rangle\):

TableauPermute[Tableau[{{1, 2}, {3}}], Psi[1, 1, 2]]

2 Psi[1, 1, 2] - Psi[1, 2, 1] - Psi[2, 1, 1]

The result is antisymmetric in the column \(\{1,3\}\) and symmetric in the row \(\{1,2\}\).

Tensor tableaux as compact states¶

TensorTableau[rows] is the symmetrized state in compact form; ToTensor expands it into the Psi basis, and TableauForm displays it as a Young grid (which renders best in a notebook front end):

ToTensor[TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {2}}]]

Psi[1, 2, 2] + Psi[2, 1, 2] - 2 Psi[2, 2, 1]

Because columns are antisymmetric, a repeated entry in a column annihilates the state. The tableau {{1,2},{1}} has 1 twice in its first column:

ToTensor[TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {1}}]]

0

Inner products and normalization¶

TableauDot is the Hermitian inner product and TableauNormalization scales to unit norm. Take \(|a\rangle = \) {{1,2},{2}}:

a = TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {2}}];
TableauDot[a, a]                       (* 6 *)
na = TableauNormalization[a]           (* TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {2}}] / Sqrt[6] *)
TableauDot[na, na]                     (* 1 *)

TensorDot / TensorNorm do the same at the level of raw Psi tensors:

TensorNorm[ToTensor[a]]                (* Sqrt[6] *)

Orthogonalizing a degenerate weight space¶

In the {1, 1} irrep the weight {0, 0} is shared by two independent states. They are not orthogonal, so — as with Gram–Schmidt — we keep the first and orthogonalize the second against it. TableauOrthogonalization[t1, t2] returns \(\{t_1,\ t_2 - \tfrac{\langle t_2,t_1\rangle}{\langle t_1,t_1\rangle}t_1\}\):

t1 = 2 TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {3}}] - TensorTableau[{{1, 3}, {2}}];
t2 = TensorTableau[{{1, 3}, {2}}] + TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {3}}];
{c, d} = TableauOrthogonalization[t1, t2];
TableauDot[c, d]                       (* 0 *)

c = 2 TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {3}}] - TensorTableau[{{1, 3}, {2}}]
d = (3 TensorTableau[{{1, 3}, {2}}]) / 2

A transition matrix element¶

Lowering the normalized \(|a\rangle\) with \(F_2\) reaches the superposition \(|b\rangle = (\,\){{1,2},{3}}\( + \){{1,3},{2}}\(\,)/\sqrt{6}\) (worked out in the guided tour). Its overlap with a {0,0} basis state is the transition matrix element:

b = TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {3}}]/Sqrt[6] + TensorTableau[{{1, 3}, {2}}]/Sqrt[6];
nd = TableauNormalization[2 TensorTableau[{{1, 2}, {3}}] - TensorTableau[{{1, 3}, {2}}]];
TableauDot[b, b]                       (* 2 *)
TableauDot[nd, b]                      (* 1/Sqrt[2]: the transition matrix element *)
